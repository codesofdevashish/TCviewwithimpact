                To model and estimate the spatial patterns of cyclone vulnerability and resulting damage, we integrate cyclone hazard dynamics, exposure (population and economic wealth) and social vulnerability into a unified spatial framework.

                1. Data integration

                All variables are mapped onto a standardised two-dimensional grid of 0.1° cells with coordinates (x, y), cut out around each storm's track.

                2. Hazard H(x, y)

                The wind field follows the Holland (1980) profile, V(r) = Vmax · [ (Rmax/r)B · exp(1 − (Rmax/r)B) ]½, driven hour by hour by the official track and intensity (NCEP TCVitals). The shape parameter B = ρ e Vmax2 / Δp (limited to 1–2.5) uses the pressure deficit Δp between the environment and the centre; the radius of maximum wind Rmax comes from TCVitals (Willoughby et al. 2006 when missing). 0.55 × the storm's forward motion is added for the asymmetry. The footprint is the highest wind each cell experiences along the track.

                3. Exposure E(x, y)

                Population from GHSL GHS-POP 2025 (EU JRC), summed into each cell. Economic value = population × national GDP per capita (PPP, World Bank), the population-weighted form of LitPop (Eberenz et al. 2020). In the risk index, E = min(1, log10(1 + people) / 4.5).

                4. Vulnerability V(x, y)

                A country-level index (0 = least, 1 = most vulnerable): the mean of scaled GDP per capita (PPP), under-5 mortality and lack of access to electricity (World Bank). This first version is deliberately simple and coarse; a gridded deprivation index (GRDI, NASA SEDAC) can replace it.

                5. Damage and risk

                Mean damage ratio f(V) = v3 / (1 + v3), v = max(V − 25.7, 0) / (74.7 − 25.7) with V in m/s (Emanuel 2011, CLIMADA default thresholds). Risk index R(x, y) = 100 · f(H) · E · V, shown in five classes from very low (< 1) to very high (> 20). It is a relative index: no money damage is estimated.

                Limitations

                • Wind only. Storm surge and rainfall flooding, which cause most deaths in the Bay of Bengal, are not modelled.
                • The damage curve is not calibrated for each region, so the index compares places and storms rather than predicting losses.
                • Vulnerability is national, so differences within a country are not captured.
                • Population is a 2025 estimate; official intensities have their own uncertainty.

                For comparison, the Global Disaster Alert and Coordination System (GDACS) publishes exposed-population estimates for every storm.
